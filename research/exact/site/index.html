Abstract

A knot can be built from unit sticks that lie along the edges of the cubic lattice ℤ3\mathbb{Z}^3. The least number of sticks it needs is its minimal lattice length. Exact values have been reported for only three knots: the trefoil needs 24 sticks, the figure-eight knot 30 and the cinquefoil 515_1 34. We prove that the knot 525_2 needs exactly 36.

The proof has three stages. (1) A normal form: if a 525_2 polygon with at most 34 sticks existed, then one particular shortest one, chosen by pushing it as far down as possible, would satisfy seven local conditions. We call these conditions the Dynamic Midwalk Obligation Neutralizer tests, DMON1–DMON7. (2) An exhaustive search: a depth-first enumeration lists every closed walk of length at most 34 that passes the DMON tests. The search prunes a partial walk only when a proved lemma shows that no completion can pass them; we call this completeness statement the PanDMONium theorem. (3) Certificates: each of the 33 000 words returned has a diagram whose Fox 77-colourings are all constant, while every diagram of 525_2 has 49 colourings. As a by-product, the search gives a new computational proof that every non-trivial lattice knot needs at least 24 sticks, so that Len(31)=24\operatorname{Len}(3_1)=24.

The mathematical steps are formalised in the Lean 4 proof assistant and checked by its kernel, with no axioms beyond Lean’s standard three. The computation, 1358 closed Boolean terms evaluated by the compiled verified program (about 2.27×10122.27\times 10^{12} search nodes), ran on GitHub’s free runners and agrees with a separate C++ implementation of the same algorithm.

1 Introduction

Take a knot and build it from unit sticks that lie along the edges of the cubic lattice: each stick joins an integer point to a neighbouring integer point, and the sticks close up into a polygon that does not touch itself. How few sticks are needed? This number, the minimal lattice length Len(K)\operatorname{Len}(K) of the knot type KK, is one of the simplest measures of how complicated a knot is. It has been studied since the 1990s, both in geometric knot theory and because lattice polygons are the standard model of ring polymers in statistical physics [5, 19, 20].

Upper bounds are easy in principle: exhibit a polygon. Simulated annealing and other stochastic searches have produced short polygons for all knots with up to ten crossings [19, 20]. Lower bounds are hard. Diao proved in 1993 that every non-trivial knot needs at least 24 sticks and that the trefoil attains this [5]. Scharein, Ishihara, Arsuaga, Diao, Shimokawa and Vazquez reported that the figure-eight knot needs 30 sticks and the cinquefoil 515_1 needs 34 [19]. For 525_2, the second knot with five crossings, a 36-stick polygon is known [19, 20], but no matching lower bound had been proved.

Theorem 1.1 (Main theorem). Len(52)=36\operatorname{Len}(5_2)=36.

The upper bound is the polygon of Figure 1. The content of the theorem is the lower bound: no lattice polygon with 34 or fewer steps is a 525_2. One cannot simply list all lattice polygons of length 34: their number grows like μn\mu^{n} with μ≈4.684\mu\approx4.684 [8, 2], which for n=34n=34 is far too many. Instead we list only the candidates for one carefully chosen shortest representative, and we rule out each candidate with a short algebraic certificate that anyone can check. The proof has three stages.

  1. A normal form (Section 3). If 525_2 had a polygon with at most 34 steps, then a particular shortest one, chosen by pushing the polygon as far down as possible and then placing it in a canonical position, would satisfy seven local conditions, the DMON tests DMON1–DMON7. They follow from two kinds of square moves, which change a polygon without changing its knot type.

  2. An exhaustive search (Section 4). A depth-first search lists every closed walk of length at most 34 that passes DMON1–DMON7. It discards a partial walk only when a proved lemma shows that no completion of it can pass them (the PanDMONium theorem, Theorem 4.1). The search visits about 2.27×10122.27\times 10^{12} partial walks and returns 33 000 words.

  3. Certificates (Section 5). For each returned word we give a regular diagram and a matrix certificate showing that every Fox 77-colouring of the diagram is constant. Every diagram of 525_2 has non-constant ones. So none of the words is a 525_2.

What has to be trusted. This is a computer-assisted proof, so we say exactly what a reader has to accept. All three stages are formalised in Lean 4 [4] (Section 7): the normal-form theorem, the completeness of the actual search program that was run, and the soundness of the certificates are theorems checked by the Lean kernel. What remains is:

  1. the short list of definitions that state the theorem;

  2. three classical facts of knot theory: (E1) a Δ\Delta-move does not change the knot type (proved in full in Theorem 2.4, but not formalised in Lean); (E2) the Wirtinger presentation of the knot group, a textbook theorem that we quote with references (used in Theorem 5.1); and (E3), used only to give names (our 36-step polygon is the knot that the tables call 525_2, and in Corollary 4.6 a 24-step polygon is the trefoil): two diagrams with the same planar-diagram code show the same knot, up to mirror image (derived in Section 6 from quoted theorems);

  3. that the compiled program evaluated 1358 closed Boolean terms correctly, and printed the correct number of returned words for each part;

  4. the records that this evaluation returned true, with those word counts: GitHub’s archived job logs (Section 8). The word counts matter only for Corollary 4.6.

In particular the pruning of the search is not trusted: every discarded branch is discarded by a test that Lean has proved can never discard a word passing the DMON tests.

2 Preliminaries

2.1 Lattice polygons

The six unit vectors of ℤ3\mathbb{Z}^3 are written 𝚎,𝚠,𝚗,𝚜,𝚞,𝚍\texttt{e},\texttt{w},\texttt{n},\texttt{s},\texttt{u},\texttt{d} (east, west, north, south, up, down) for ±x\pm x, ±y\pm y, ±z\pm z. For a letter aa we write −a-a or rev(a)\operatorname{rev}(a) for the opposite letter. Two letters are perpendicular if they lie on different axes.

Definition 2.1. A word w=w0w1⋯wn−1w=w_0w_1\cdots w_{n-1} in these letters describes the walk with vertices v0=0v_0=0 and vi+1=vi+wiv_{i+1}=v_i+w_i. It is a polygon word if n≥4n\ge 4, vn=0v_n=0, and v0,…,vn−1v_0,\dots,v_{n-1} are pairwise distinct. Its polygon |w||w| is the union of the nn unit segments [vi,vi+1][v_i,v_{i+1}] (indices mod nn).

Two distinct unit lattice segments meet in at most one common endpoint (if they are perpendicular, a common point has integer coordinates on both axes, so it is an endpoint; parallel segments are disjoint or collinear, and collinear unit segments between consecutive integers meet only at a shared endpoint). So |w||w| is a simple closed curve made of nn segments. Every lattice polygon is a translate of |w||w| for some polygon word ww: start at any vertex and read off the steps.

Lemma 2.2 (Parity; Lean: isPolygon_length_even). Every polygon word has even length.

Proof. Since vn=v0v_n=v_0, for each axis the number of positive steps equals the number of negative steps. So the number of letters on each axis is even, and so is their total nn. ◻

2.2 Knots and Δ\Delta-moves

A knot is a simple closed curve in ℝ3\mathbb{R}^3; all our knots are polygonal. Two knots KK, K′K' are equivalent (have the same knot type) if there is an orientation-preserving homeomorphism hh of ℝ3\mathbb{R}^3 with h(K)=K′h(K)=K'. The mirror image K*K^* of KK is its image under a reflection of ℝ3\mathbb{R}^3. The unknot is the knot type of a round circle, or equivalently of the boundary of a unit square (a flat convex polygon); a knot is non-trivial, or knotted, if it is not the unknot. The mirror image of the unknot is the unknot (a reflection maps a flat polygon to a flat polygon). We take knot types without orientation. For polygonal knots there is a simple, local way to change a knot without changing its type.

Definition 2.3 (Δ\Delta-move). Let KK be a polygonal knot with an edge [a,b][a,b], and let cc be a point not on the line abab. If the closed triangle Δ=abc\Delta=abc meets KK only in the edge [a,b][a,b], then replacing [a,b][a,b] by the two edges [a,c]∪[c,b][a,c]\cup[c,b] is a Δ\Delta-move. The reverse replacement, allowed when Δ\Delta meets KK only in [a,c]∪[c,b][a,c]\cup[c,b], is also a Δ\Delta-move (Figure 2).

The intuition: slide the edge [a,b][a,b] across the flat triangle onto the two other sides. Nothing is in the way, so nothing is cut or passed through.

Definition 2.5. Len(K)\operatorname{Len}(K) is the least nn such that some lattice polygon with nn edges has knot type KK.

A reflection of ℤ3\mathbb{Z}^3 (for example (x,y,z)↦(−x,y,z)(x,y,z)\mapsto(-x,y,z)) maps lattice polygons to lattice polygons with the same number of edges and maps a knot to its mirror image. So Len(K*)=Len(K)\operatorname{Len}(K^*)=\operatorname{Len}(K).

2.3 Diagrams

A linear projection of a knot onto a plane is regular if the image has only finitely many multiple points, all of them transverse double points, and no vertex is mapped onto a double point. A double point is a crossing. At each crossing one strand, the over-strand, is nearer the viewer. If we cut the image at every under-passage, the pieces are the arcs of the diagram. So at every crossing three arcs meet: the over-arc, the incoming under-arc and the outgoing under-arc (two of them may coincide). We use orientations only to name “incoming” and “outgoing”.

A PD code (planar diagram code) describes an oriented diagram with cc crossings. Cut the knot at the 2c2c passages through crossings (over and under); the resulting pieces are the edges, numbered 1,…,2c1,\dots,2c along the orientation. Each crossing is written X[a,b,c,d]X[a,b,c,d]: the four edge ends at the crossing in counter-clockwise order, starting with the incoming edge of the under-strand. So aa and cc are the under-strand (in and out) and bb, dd are the over-strand. For the diagram of a lattice polygon we orient the knot along its word and number the edges starting at v0v_0: edge 11 is the piece that begins at the first passage after v0v_0, so the piece through v0v_0 is edge 2c2c. Renumbering the edges by a cyclic shift, or reversing the orientation, gives another PD code of the same diagram (a relabelling). Switching every crossing turns X[a,b,c,d]X[a,b,c,d] into X[b,c,d,a]X[b,c,d,a]: the over-strand becomes the under-strand, and its incoming edge is bb or dd, whichever comes first counter-clockwise after the old under-strand.

2.4 Fox colourings

Definition 2.6 (Fox [6]; see also [15], [10, §§3.2–3.3]). Let p≥3p\ge3 be an odd number. A Fox pp-colouring of a diagram assigns to each arc an element of ℤ/p\mathbb{Z}/p so that at every crossing 2⋅(over-arc)≡(incoming under-arc)+(outgoing under-arc)(modp).2\cdot(\text{over-arc}) \equiv (\text{incoming under-arc}) + (\text{outgoing under-arc}) \pmod p.

Figure 3 shows the rule and an example. Two facts make colourings useful.

  1. Constant colourings always exist. If every arc has the colour aa, then at every crossing 2a≡a+a2a\equiv a+a. So every diagram has at least pp colourings.

  2. The number of colourings depends only on the knot type, and it is the same for KK and K*K^* [6, 15], [10, §3.3]. We prove this for p=7p=7 in Theorem 5.1, the only case we need; the same proof works for every odd prime pp.

So a non-constant colouring in one diagram of KK proves that KK is knotted, and that KK differs from every knot whose diagrams have only constant colourings. For an odd prime pp, a knot has a non-constant pp-colouring exactly when pp divides its determinant [10, §§3.3–3.4], [15] (we quote this only to explain the choice p=7p=7; the proof does not use it). The determinants of 31,41,51,523_1,4_1,5_1,5_2 are 3,5,5,73,5,5,7. This is why p=7p=7 separates 525_2 from all other knots with at most five crossings: the standard diagram of 525_2 has 4949 colourings (Lemma 5.3), while the unknot, the trefoil, the figure-eight knot and 515_1 have only the 77 constant ones.

3 Stage one: a normal form for a shortest polygon

In this section TT is a knot class: a set of words that behaves like “the words of a given knot type, up to mirror image”. Precisely, TT is a knot class (Lean: KnotClass) if

  1. every member of TT is a polygon word;

  2. TT is closed under rotation of the word (Lemma 3.4(a));

  3. TT is closed under reversal of the word (Lemma 3.4(b));

  4. TT is closed under the 4848 lattice symmetries (Lemma 3.4(c));

  5. TT is closed under the corner flips allowed by Lemma 3.2;

  6. TT is closed under the U-turn removals allowed by Lemma 3.3.

Corollary 3.5 below shows that the main example is a knot class: T52={w:w is a polygon word and |w| has knot type 52 or 52*}.T_{5_2}=\{w : w \text{ is a polygon word and } |w| \text{ has knot type } 5_2 \text{ or } 5_2^*\}. We assume that TT contains a word of length at most 34, and we show that it then contains a word that passes seven local tests. The tools are two moves across a unit square.

3.2 Pushing the polygon down: the potentials ψZ\psi_Z and ψY\psi_Y

Among the shortest words in TT we want to pick one that is as low as possible, as if gravity pulled the polygon towards −z-z, and then, with height as a tie-breaker, towards −y-y. To make “as low as possible” precise we use a number that measures how high a polygon sits.

Definition 3.6 (Potentials). For a polygon word ww with vertices v0,…,vn−1v_0,\dots,v_{n-1}, write z(v)z(v) and y(v)y(v) for the zz- and yy-coordinates of a point vv, and let zmin=min0≤i<nz(vi),ymin=min0≤i<ny(vi).z_{\min}=\min_{0\le i<n} z(v_i), \qquad y_{\min}=\min_{0\le i<n} y(v_i). The height potential and the depth potential of ww are ψZ(w)=∑i=0n−1(z(vi)−zmin),ψY(w)=∑i=0n−1(y(vi)−ymin).\psi_Z(w)=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\bigl(z(v_i)-z_{\min}\bigr), \qquad \psi_Y(w)=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\bigl(y(v_i)-y_{\min}\bigr).

Each term z(vi)−zminz(v_i)-z_{\min} is the height of the vertex viv_i above the lowest level of the polygon. So ψZ\psi_Z is the total height of all vertices above the floor, a whole number ≥0\ge0; think of it as the potential energy of nn equal weights placed at the vertices. Subtracting zminz_{\min} makes ψZ\psi_Z independent of where the polygon sits: a translation changes every z(vi)z(v_i) and zminz_{\min} by the same amount. ψY\psi_Y is the same with yy in place of zz. Figure 6 computes ψZ\psi_Z for a small example. For instance, the lattice points 0,1,…,n0,1,\dots,n of a vertical segment [0,n][0,n] have potential 0+1+…+n=n(n+1)/20+1+\dots+n=n(n+1)/2.

Other quantities could be called potentials, and comparing them shows why we use this one. The sum ∑i1=n\sum_i 1=n counts vertices and does not see height at all. The sum ∑iz(vi)\sum_i z(v_i), without zminz_{\min}, changes when the polygon is translated, and rotating the word translates it. The sum ∑i(z(vi)−zmin)2\sum_i(z(v_i)-z_{\min})^2 would also work, since lowering a vertex from height h≥1h\ge1 to h−1h-1 lowers it by 2h−1≥12h-1\ge1; but with ψZ\psi_Z every downward flip lowers the potential by exactly 11, which keeps the bookkeeping in Lemma 3.10 simple.

Lemma 3.7 (Invariance of the potentials). Rotation and reversal of the word (Lemma 3.4a,b) do not change ψZ\psi_Z or ψY\psi_Y, and they do not change the letter counts.

Proof. Rotation translates the vertex set by −v1-v_1, and the potentials are translation-invariant. Reversal visits the same vertex set. Both moves keep the multiset of letters up to replacing aa by −a-a, so the number of letters on each axis is unchanged. ◻

3.3 The choice of the polygon

Let TT be a knot class that contains a word of length at most 34. We choose a word w∈Tw\in T in four steps.

  1. Shortest. Let mm be the least length of a word in TT. Then m≤34m\le34 and mm is even (Lemma 2.2).

  2. Sorted axes. Let #x(w),#y(w),#z(w)\#_x(w),\#_y(w),\#_z(w) be the numbers of letters of ww on each axis. Take any word u∈Tu\in T of length mm.   3. Pushed down. Let w*∈Ww^*\in W have the least ψZ\psi_Z among the words of WW, and among those, the least ψY\psi_Y.

  4. Canonical position. Rotate w*w^* (Lemma 3.4a, repeatedly) so that v0v_0 is the vertex that is least in the order “first by zz, then by yy, then by xx”. Then, if w0w_0 is larger than −wn−1-w_{n-1} in the order 𝚎<𝚠<𝚗<𝚜<𝚞<𝚍\texttt{e}<\texttt{w}<\texttt{n}<\texttt{s}<\texttt{u}<\texttt{d}, replace the word by its reversal. Call the result ww.

By Lemma 3.7, Step 4 keeps the word in WW with the same ψZ\psi_Z and ψY\psi_Y. So ww is also a minimiser in Step 3. The next two lemmas show what this choice forces.

3.4 Curtains

A U-turn a,b,−aa,b,-a can always be removed (Lemma 3.3), so a shortest word has none. A curtain is a stretched U-turn: a few corner flips turn it into a U-turn, as long as the points behind it are free. So a shortest word cannot have a curtain with all those points free.

Definition 3.8 (Curtain). A curtain in a polygon word is a stretch of k+2k+2 cyclically consecutive letters, 1≤k≤n−21\le k\le n-2, of the form a,s1,…,sk,−aa,s_1,\dots,s_k,-a, where s1,…,sks_1,\dots,s_k are perpendicular to aa. If the stretch starts at letter ii, its interior points are vi+j−av_{i+j}-a for 2≤j≤k2\le j\le k (indices mod nn).

For k=1k=1 the curtain is a U-turn and has no interior points, so the condition “some interior point is a vertex” (DMON6 below) cannot hold: DMON6 forbids every U-turn.

So a curtain goes one step in direction aa, wanders kk steps sideways, and comes back with −a-a. Its interior points lie one step “behind” the sideways stretch, as in Figure 7a.

Lemma 3.9 (Curtain lemma; DMON6). If m≥6m\ge6, then in every word of TT of length mm, every curtain has an interior point that is a vertex.

Rotate the word so that the curtain starts at letter 00 (this translates all vertices by the same vector, so the assumption still holds, and the word stays in TT). We claim that before flip number t+1t+1 (0≤t≤k−20\le t\le k-2) the walk is v0,v2−a,v3−a,…,vt+1−a,vt+1,vt+2,…v_0,\ v_2-a,\ v_3-a,\ \dots,\ v_{t+1}-a,\ v_{t+1},\ v_{t+2},\ \dots with letters s1,…,st,a,st+1,…s_1,\dots,s_t,a,s_{t+1},\dots. 3.6 The DMON tests and the normal-form theorem

We can now state the seven conditions. The name, Dynamic Midwalk Obligation Neutralizer, refers to the way the search uses them (Section 4): DMON6 and DMON7 create obligations in the middle of a walk, which the rest of the walk must discharge.

Definition 3.11 (The DMON tests). A word w=w0⋯wn−1w=w_0\cdots w_{n-1} with vertices v0=0,v1,…,vnv_0=0,v_1,\dots,v_n passes the DMON tests if:

DMON1 (closed walk).

n≥4n\ge4, every wiw_i is one of the six letters, and vn=0v_n=0: the walk returns to its start.

DMON2 (self-avoiding).

The vertices v0,…,vn−1v_0,\dots,v_{n-1} are pairwise distinct.

DMON3 (lowest start).

Every vertex viv_i, 1≤i<n1\le i<n, is greater than v0=0v_0=0 in the order “first zz, then yy, then xx”.

DMON4 (direction of travel).

w0<−wn−1w_0<-w_{n-1} in the order 𝚎<𝚠<𝚗<𝚜<𝚞<𝚍\texttt{e}<\texttt{w}<\texttt{n}<\texttt{s}<\texttt{u}<\texttt{d}.

DMON5 (sorted axes).

#z(w)≤#y(w)≤#x(w)\#_z(w)\le\#_y(w)\le\#_x(w).

DMON6 (curtains).

Every curtain has an interior point that is a vertex.

DMON7 (corners).

At every vertex viv_i, 1≤i<n1\le i<n, whose two letters form one of the four patterns of Lemma 3.10, the point vi−1+wiv_{i-1}+w_i is a vertex.

DMON1 and DMON2 together say that ww is a polygon word. DMON3, DMON4 and DMON5 fix the position of the polygon, so that the search meets each polygon in only one of its many positions. DMON3 picks the starting vertex: the lowest one. A polygon through its lowest vertex can still be read in two directions, and DMON4 picks one of them (Figure 9). Indeed, by DMON3 both neighbours v1v_1 and vn−1v_{n-1} of the origin are greater than it, so w0w_0 and −wn−1-w_{n-1} (the steps from the origin to v1v_1 and to vn−1v_{n-1}) both lie in {𝚎,𝚗,𝚞}\{\texttt{e},\texttt{n},\texttt{u}\}, and they differ because v1≠vn−1v_1\ne v_{n-1}. Reading the polygon backwards exchanges them. So exactly one of the two readings has w0<−wn−1w_0<-w_{n-1}. Without DMON4 the search would find every polygon twice and take about twice as long.

DMON6 and DMON7 are different in kind. Each has the form “some point of a small finite set is a vertex”, and the set is known as soon as a few consecutive letters are known. The vertex that satisfies the condition, however, may be visited much later in the walk. This is what the search exploits in Section 4.2.

Theorem 3.12 (Normal form; Lean: exists_survivor). Let TT be a knot class. If TT contains a word uu, then TT contains a word ww, not longer than uu, that has length 44 or passes DMON1–DMON7.

Proof. Let mm be the least length of a word in TT, so mm is at most the length of uu. If m=4m=4, any word of length 44 in TT will do. Otherwise m≥6m\ge6 by Lemma 2.2. Let ww be the word chosen in Section 3.3. It is in TT, so it is a polygon word: DMON1 and DMON2 hold. DMON5 holds because w∈Ww\in W. In Step 4, v0v_0 is the least vertex in the order (z,y,x)(z,y,x) and the vertices are distinct, so every other vertex is greater: DMON3. The reversal in Step 4 makes w0<−wn−1w_0<-w_{n-1} (they are distinct, as shown above), and reversal keeps v0v_0: DMON4. DMON6 is Lemma 3.9 (all words of WW have length m≥6m\ge6), and DMON7 is Lemma 3.10. ◻

The words of length 44 are the unit squares. A unit square bounds a flat disc, so it is an unknot and is not in T52T_{5_2} or T≠0T_{\ne0}; in Lean, square_ok checks that every unit square passes the colouring test of Section 5.3.

4 Stage two: the search

The search lists, for each even n≤34n\le34, every word of length nn that passes the DMON tests (and possibly some that do not; extra words do no harm, they are ruled out in Section 5). It builds walks from the origin one step at a time, trying the six letters in the fixed order 𝚎,𝚠,𝚗,𝚜,𝚞,𝚍\texttt{e},\texttt{w},\texttt{n},\texttt{s},\texttt{u},\texttt{d}, and it abandons a partial walk as soon as a test shows that no completion to a closed walk of length nn can pass DMON1–DMON7. This section describes the tests, explains why each is sound, and then proves that the search misses nothing.

4.1 The state of the search

When the search has built the prefix w0⋯wi−1w_0\cdots w_{i-1} and is about to try a letter dd as wiw_i, it knows:

  • the occupied points O={v0,…,vi}O=\{v_0,\dots,v_i\} and the head viv_i;

  • the number R=n−iR=n-i of steps still to be taken, including the one being tried;

  • the number of letters used so far on each axis, and, for each axis, the first and the most recent letter on that axis together with their positions;

  • a list Π\Pi of pending obligations, described next.

4.2 Obligations

Consider DMON6 while the walk is being built. The moment the walk closes a curtain, by taking a step −a-a that answers an earlier step aa, the interior points of the curtain are known. DMON6 says that one of them must be a vertex. But the walk need not have visited it yet: the condition can still be met by a later step. So the search can neither accept nor reject the walk at this moment. Instead it records the interior points that are still free as an obligation: a list of lattice points, the sites, at least one of which the rest of the walk must visit (Figure 10). DMON7 is handled in the same way, with an obligation that has a single site v′v'.

Obligations are what make the search fast. A pending obligation constrains the rest of the walk: it must still be possible to visit a site and come home in the remaining steps, and the site must have room to be passed through. These are the reach and span tests below. When the walk visits a site, every obligation containing that site is discharged and removed. When the walk closes, no obligation may remain.

Why reach and span are the right tests. An obligation says “some site must be a later vertex”. 5.3 Certificates that a diagram has only constant colourings

For each returned word ww, the program colorCheck produces a regular diagram of |w||w| and proves that all its Fox 77-colourings are constant. We describe each part, and then work through an example.

The projection. Fix a direction D=(Dx,Dy,Dz)∈ℤ3D=(D_x,D_y,D_z)\in\mathbb{Z}^3 with no zero coordinate. A point p=(x,y,z)p=(x,y,z) is drawn at πD(p)=(Dzx−Dxz,Dzy−Dyz)∈ℤ2\pi_D(p)=(D_zx-D_xz,\ D_zy-D_yz)\in\mathbb{Z}^2 and has height hD(p)=Dxx+Dyy+Dzzh_D(p)=D_xx+D_yy+D_zz. The map πD\pi_D is linear with kernel spanned by DD, so it is the projection along DD followed by a linear change of coordinates in the plane; it is chosen because it maps lattice points to integer points, so that all computations are exact. At a crossing, the strand with the larger height (nearer a viewer far away in direction +D+D) is the over-strand.

Regularity. The program checks, with exact rational arithmetic, that no edge is mapped to a point; that two edges sharing a vertex have images meeting only in the image of that vertex; that two edges sharing no vertex have disjoint images, or images that cross in a single point interior to both, with different heights there; and that no two crossing points coincide. These are exactly the conditions for a regular projection of a polygon.

Arcs. Walking along |w||w| from v0v_0, list the under-passages in order: u0,…,uc−1u_0,\dots,u_{c-1}. Arc ee runs from ueu_e to ue+1u_{e+1} (indices mod cc). At the crossing with under-passage ueu_e the incoming under-arc is e−1e-1 and the outgoing one is ee.

The program finds, by Gaussian elimination mod 77, a (c−1)×c(c-1)\times c matrix LL such that LF≡E(mod7),LF\equiv E\pmod 7, where row kk of EE is ek−e0e_k-e_0 (k=1,…,c−1k=1,\dots,c-1), and eke_k is the kk-th unit row vector. 7 The formalisation

The Lean development (plain Lean 4.25, no Mathlib, about 4500 lines) proves one main theorem:

theorem no_short (T : List Nat → Prop) (hT : KnotClass T) (hTop : TopHyp T)
    (hComp : CompCert) : ∀ w, T w → 36 ≤ w.length

Here TT is any set of words. KnotClass T says that TT is a knot class, (K1)–(K6) of Section 3. TopHyp T says that every regular diagram of a member of TT has a non-constant Fox 77-colouring. CompCert says that every word returned by every part of the search passes the colouring test. For T=T52T=T_{5_2} the first two hypotheses are Corollary 3.5 and Theorem 5.1 with Lemma 5.3; the third is the computation.

Table 2 lists every result of the paper with its Lean name and what it depends on. Read from the bottom up, it is the dependency chain of the proof: no_short combines the normal form (which needs only KnotClass), PanDMONium (which needs only DMON1–DMON7, called Survive in Lean), the certificate lemma and the computation.

The statement. To check the theorem one must read the definitions (polygon words, the moves and KnotClass, regular diagrams and Fox equations, TopHyp, CompCert) and the statement of no_short. Everything else is checked by the kernel. #print axioms reports only propext, Classical.choice and Quot.sound. The sources contain no sorry, native_decide, implemented_by or extern, and a GitHub workflow rebuilds everything from a clean checkout and prints the axioms.

The program is the proof object. Theorem 4.1 is proved about the very program that was run. The search is written in a functional style that the kernel can reason about: walk data in immutable lists, explicit structural recursion on the remaining steps, and a single mutable occupancy array, which the compiler updates in place. It is a line-by-line port, by the same author, of a C++ program. Where the runs are unsplit (n≤26n\le26), both programs visit exactly the same number of nodes (Section 9).

8 The computation

By a one-line Lean lemma (compCert_of_checks), CompCert is equivalent to the truth of 1358 closed Boolean terms 𝚌𝚑𝚎𝚌𝚔𝙿𝚊𝚛𝚝nr\mathtt{checkPart}\ n\ r: for each even n≤34n\le34 and each part rr, run the search for length nn restricted to part rr and check every returned word with the certificate program. The executable checkpart evaluates exactly this term, prints the words and a final line RESULT … ok=true, and exits with status 00 if and only if the term is true.

Where it ran. The total is about 2.27×10122.27\times 10^{12} search nodes. Every part ran on GitHub Actions, which offers twenty free 4-core runners at a time for public repositories. At about 5×1055\times10^5 nodes per second per core, most parts of length 34 took between 40 and 90 minutes (median one hour). Length 34 used about 1060 core-hours, about sixteen hours of wall time on the free runners.

Provenance. A part is accepted only if its log records exit status 00, its output ends with the RESULT line for that part with the right number of words before it, the output’s SHA-256 matches the logged one, and the program was built from sources identical to the current ones. The record that a part was evaluated on GitHub is GitHub’s own job log, which the repository owner cannot edit: the committed log must be byte-identical to the block that the job printed, in a successful job of a run whose head commit has the frozen sources. The job logs of all proof runs are archived in the repository (GitHub keeps them only for a limited time). The output of each part is deterministic, so anyone can re-run any part and compare hashes. Every part was in fact evaluated at least twice, in two separate series of GitHub runs; in addition, 8 random parts were re-run in our container and 6 with a build whose code was compiled by gcc instead of Lean’s bundled clang. All outputs agree byte for byte.

What is trusted. That the compiled program evaluated the 1358 Booleans correctly. This rests on the Lean compiler and the compiled implementations in its core library (arrays, big natural numbers, list sorting), its C back end, the runtime and the hardware: the usual trust model for verified computations too large for the kernel. It is backed by the separate C++ program (Section 9).

9 Independent cross-checks

None of the following is needed for the proof; each one tests it.

  • A second implementation. The C++ enumerator in its cross-check mode implements the same algorithm separately (the Lean search is a port of it by the same author). For every length it returns the same set of words, and for unsplit runs (n≤26n\le26) exactly the same number of nodes. For split runs the two programs split differently, so only the words are compared.

  • Independent re-implementations of the definitions. A Python implementation of the projection and the colouring count finds exactly 77 colourings for every returned word, and a Python implementation of DMON1–DMON7, written from the Lean definitions alone, accepts every returned word.

  • The trefoil census. Of the 56 words returned at length 24, 50 have non-constant 33-colourings, so they are knotted (the other 6 have only constant 33-colourings). The closure of these 50 under corner flips and lattice symmetries is the full set of 3328 shortest trefoil polygons known from [20]. A missing branch of the search would show up as a missing class.

10 Discussion

The value Len(52)=36\operatorname{Len}(5_2)=36 is, to our knowledge, the first exact minimal lattice length beyond the three reported before, and the first whose mathematical steps are checked by a proof assistant. Reaching length 40, which would settle Len(61)\operatorname{Len}(6_1), Len(62)\operatorname{Len}(6_2), Len(63)\operatorname{Len}(6_3) and Len(31#31)\operatorname{Len}(3_1\#3_1), all believed to be 40, needs roughly 5050 times the present computation, or stronger DMON tests. Finding such tests (new conditions that a well-chosen shortest polygon must satisfy, and that can be checked in the middle of a walk) is the most direct way to push the method further.
