Abstract

A knot drawn as a closed walk on the cubic lattice ℤ³ needs a certain least number of unit steps, its minimal lattice length. This number was known for only three knots: the trefoil needs 24 steps, the figure-eight knot 30 and the cinquefoil 5₁ needs 34. For every other knot only upper bounds were known. We prove that the knot 5₂ needs exactly 36 steps.We show that the knot 5₂ can be built from 36 steps, and give a proof that it cannot be built from fewer; its last computer checks are still running.

The proof has three stages. (1) A normal form: a shortest 5₂ polygon can be moved, by square moves that change neither the knot nor the length, into a position where seven local conditions hold. (2) An exhaustive search: a depth-first enumeration of every closed walk of length at most 34 that satisfies them, pruned only by tests that are theorems. (3) Certificates: every one of the … words the search returns has a regular diagram whose Fox 7-colourings are all constant, while every diagram of 5₂ has 49 of them.

Every step is formalised in Lean 4: the normal form, the completeness of the search program, the soundness of the certificates and the identification of the 36-step example are kernel-checked, with no axioms beyond Lean's standard three. The computation, … closed Boolean terms evaluated by the compiled verified program (… search nodes so far), ran in a small container and on GitHub's free runners, and was cross-checked node for node against an independent C++ program.

Background

What a lattice knot is, what was known before this work, and why the obvious approach, trying every polygon, cannot work.

Take a knot and build it from unit sticks that lie along the edges of the cubic lattice: every stick runs from one integer point to a neighbouring one, and the sticks close up into a polygon that does not touch itself. How few sticks are needed? This number, the minimal lattice length Len(K) of the knot type K, is one of the simplest measures of how complicated a knot is. It has been studied since the 1990s, both as a question in geometric knot theory and because lattice polygons are the standard model of ring polymers in statistical physics.

Upper bounds are easy in principle: exhibit a polygon. Simulated annealing and other stochastic searches have produced short polygons for every knot with up to ten crossings, and these are believed to be optimal for small knots. Lower bounds are hard: before this work they were known for three knots only.

A knot made of sticks

Each letter is one unit step: e and w go east and west, n and s north and south, u and d up and down. The walk drawn here never touches itself and comes back to where it started, so the sticks close into a knot.

The question for every knot is the same: how few sticks will do?

Commentary

A polygon word w of length n gives vertices v₀ = 0, vi+1 = vi + wi, with vn = 0 and v₀ … vn−1 distinct. Its edges form a simple closed curve. Every polygon word has even length, since each axis is crossed equally often both ways. Len(K) = Len(K*), because a reflection of ℤ³ preserves length.

Three knots had been solved

Finding a short polygon is a matter of luck and patience. Proving that nothing shorter exists is the hard part. Until now that had been done for three knots: the trefoil needs 24 steps, the figure-eight knot 30 and the knot 5₁ needs 34.

For 5₂, the next knot in the tables, a 36-step polygon was known. Whether 34 steps could do was open.

Commentary

Diao (1993) proved Len(3₁) = 24, and that every non-trivial knot needs at least 24 steps. Scharein, Ishihara, Arsuaga, Diao, Shimokawa and Vazquez (2009) reported Len(4₁) = 30 and Len(5₁) = 34. Upper bounds for knots up to ten crossings come from stochastic searches (Scharein et al. 2009; Janse van Rensburg and Rechnitzer 2011).

Why not just try them all?

Lattice polygons with 34 steps number about a billion billion. Checking one every nanosecond would take thirty years.

The search visited … partial walks in all and still provably missed nothing. It never looks at a polygon unless that polygon could be the one special shortest 5₂ described in the next chapters.

Commentary

The number of n-step self-avoiding polygons in ℤ³ grows like μⁿ with μ ≈ 4.684, which at n = 34 is of order 10¹⁸ up to translation. The search counts only canonical prefixes satisfying conditions C1–C7, pruned by tests that are theorems about those conditions.

Methodology

The upper bound is one polygon. The content of the theorem is the lower bound: no lattice polygon with 34 or fewer steps is a 5₂. There are of the order of 10¹⁸ lattice polygons of length 34, far too many to enumerate. The proof enumerates only candidates for one carefully chosen shortest representative, and rules out each candidate by a short, independently checkable certificate.

  1. A normal form. If 5₂ had a polygon with at most 34 steps, then a particular one of the shortest such polygons, pushed as far down as possible and put in a canonical position, would satisfy seven local conditions C1–C7. They come from two square moves that never change the knot.
  2. An exhaustive search. A depth-first search lists every closed walk of length at most 34 that satisfies C1–C7. It discards a partial walk only when a test shows that no completion can satisfy them; each test is a theorem. It has visited … partial walks and returned … words so far.
  3. Certificates. Each returned word comes with a regular diagram and a matrix certificate showing that every Fox 7-colouring of that diagram is constant. Every diagram of 5₂ has non-constant ones, so none of the words is a 5₂.

What one must take on trust

Very little. All three stages are proved in Lean 4, and the pruning of the search is not trusted: every discarded branch is discarded by a test that Lean has proved can never discard a 5₂. What remains is (i) a short list of definitions that state the theorem, (ii) two classical facts of knot theory (Δ-moves preserve knot type; the Wirtinger presentation of the knot group), and (iii) that the compiled program evaluated … true-or-false questions correctly, which an independent C++ program cross-checks node for node.

What is not used: earlier versions of this search, run to length 36, used a plane-crossing lemma valid only for prime knots and a computer-checked lemma about short knotted arcs. They made it about five times faster. The final search uses only C1–C7, which hold for every knot type.

Stage 1 · Normal form

Suppose some polygon of type 5₂ or its mirror image has at most 34 steps. Choose one shortest polygon very carefully, and the choice forces seven local conditions. The tools are two moves across a unit square.

Theorem (normal form; Lean: exists_survivor)

If a polygon of type 5₂ or 5₂* has at most 34 steps, then some polygon word w of the same type with |w| ≤ 34 is a unit square or satisfies:

C1–C2
w is a polygon word: a closed walk that never meets itself.
C3
every vertex other than v₀ is greater than v₀ = 0 in the order (z, y, x).
C4
the first letter is smaller than the reverse of the last, in the order e < n < u.
C5
#z ≤ #y ≤ #x letters.
C6
curtains: for every step a followed by k ≥ 1 perpendicular steps and then −a, some point vi+j − a, 2 ≤ j ≤ k, is a vertex.
C7
corners: at every vertex whose corner a flip would move down, or towards −y at constant height, the fourth corner of the square is a vertex.

Unit squares are unknots, so the colouring test excludes them. The point is that C1–C7 are local: a search can test them on partial walks, long before the walk closes.

Two moves that never change the knot

Flip a corner. Two perpendicular steps can swap order, so the corner jumps across its little square. That is allowed when the fourth corner is empty.

Cut a U-turn. Three sides of a square can be replaced by the fourth. The knot stays the same and loses two sticks.

Click a corner to flip it. Click a U-turn to cut it.

Commentary

A unit lattice segment meets a closed unit lattice square in nothing, a corner or a whole side. So in both moves the square meets the polygon only in the edges being replaced, and each move is two Δ-moves across the square's triangles (Lean: KnotClass.flip, KnotClass.uturn).

The curtain

Suppose the polygon steps up, wanders sideways, then steps down. If none of the ringed points under the sideways stretch is occupied, flips slide the up-step along until it meets the down-step, and a U-turn cut removes both.

So a shortest polygon always has a vertex under every such curtain. Search can test that while the walk is still being built.

Commentary

C6. For every letter a followed by k ≥ 1 perpendicular letters and then −a, some point vi+j − a with 2 ≤ j ≤ k is a vertex. Proved in Lean (curtainOK_of_min) by induction on k, one flip and a rotation at a time.

Choose the lowest one

Among all the shortest polygons of the knot, take the one whose vertices sit lowest on average. Then no corner can be flipped downwards, because that would make it lower still. Every corner that points the wrong way has its fourth corner occupied.

Seven rules for a suspect

Put the lowest polygon in a standard position: its lowest vertex at the origin, a fixed direction of travel, the axes in a fixed order. Then it obeys seven local rules.

If 5₂ had a polygon with 34 steps or fewer, some such polygon would obey all seven. So the search only has to find every walk that obeys them.

Commentary

C1–C2 polygon word; C3 root least in (z, y, x); C4 w₀ < rev(wn−1); C5 #z ≤ #y ≤ #x; C6 curtains; C7 corners. Theorem exists_survivor: every knot class closed under the moves contains, no longer than any given member, a unit square or a word with all seven.

Stage 2 · Search

The search builds walks from the origin one step at a time, trying the six letters in a fixed order, and backtracks as soon as a test shows that the partial walk cannot be completed to a closed walk of length n satisfying C1–C7.

The key device is the obligation. When the walk completes a curtain (C6) or turns a corner covered by C7, the condition neither fails nor succeeds yet: it says that one of a few specific lattice points must eventually be a vertex. The search records these points as a pending obligation. An obligation is met when the walk later visits one of its points, and every pending obligation constrains the rest of the walk.

The six tests

Position
The next vertex is new, greater than the origin in (z, y, x) order (C3), and within distance R − 1 of the origin, where R is the number of steps left.
No U-turns
A step may not reverse the step two places before it (C6 with k = 1).
Axis counts
The counts so far must still be completable to #z ≤ #y ≤ #x (C5).
Reach
Every pending obligation has a point s that can be reached and left in time: the head can walk to s and from s back to the origin within the remaining steps, and s has two neighbours that are free, the head or the origin.
Span
The rest of the walk must visit all single-point obligations; on each axis it must sweep the interval spanned by them, the head and the origin, and the sum over the three axes must not exceed R − 1.
Closing
At the last step the walk closes at the origin, no obligation is pending, the curtains that wrap around the origin are satisfied, and C4 and C5 hold.

Theorem (completeness; Lean: search_complete)

Every word w with |w| ≤ 34 that satisfies C1–C7 is returned by the search.

The heart of the proof is an invariant: every pending obligation contains a point that is a later vertex of w. A new obligation is created from an instance of C6 or C7 for w, which names a vertex of w; if that vertex were already visited, the obligation would have been met at once. Given the invariant, the reach test passes along w, because w itself walks to that vertex and home again in time, and the span test passes because w's remaining steps visit all those vertices. The other tests follow directly from C1–C7.

A search that keeps its promises

The search grows walks one step at a time and backs off the moment a walk cannot be finished legally. When a walk completes a curtain or turns a corner, a rule is not yet broken but owed: some point must become a vertex later. The search writes that down as a promise.

A walk dies when it cannot keep a promise in the steps it has left. Lean checks that the real 5₂, if it existed, could never be cut off this way.

Commentary

Tests: self-avoidance, root order, distance home, no U-turn, axis counts, reach (each obligation has a site with |head − s|₁ + |s|₁ ≤ R − 1 and two usable neighbours), span (per-axis excursion bound), closing. Invariant: every pending obligation holds a later vertex of the word. Lean: search_complete.

Making of. Earlier runs used two more lemmas, one valid only for prime knots, and were five times faster. We removed both so that the proof would need nothing we could not check.

Stage 3 · Colourings

Each returned word is a lattice polygon of some knot type. We do not need to know which; only that it is not 5₂. Fox 7-colourings suffice, and they come with a certificate that is trivial to check.

Cut a regular diagram at every under-passage into arcs. A Fox p-colouring gives each arc a number mod p so that at every crossing twice the over-arc equals the sum of the two under-arcs. Constant assignments are always colourings. For p = 7 every diagram of 5₂ has 49 colourings, while the unknot, the trefoil, the figure-eight knot and 5₁ have only the 7 constant ones.

Why the count is an invariant (Lean hypothesis TopHyp)

Let the dihedral group D₇ act on ℤ/7 by t ↦ ±t + c; its reflections ra(t) = 2a − t satisfy rbrarb = r2b−a. In the Wirtinger presentation of the knot group read from a diagram, a homomorphism to D₇ that sends meridians to reflections sends each arc to some ra, and the crossing relation holds exactly when aout = 2aover − ain: the Fox equation. So colourings are such homomorphisms, and their number depends only on the knot group, also for the mirror image.

49 for 5₂

In the standard diagram, with PD code [1,5,2,4], [3,9,4,8], [5,1,6,10], [7,3,8,2], [9,7,10,6], the arcs are A = {2,3}, B = {4,5}, C = {6,7}, D = {8,9}, E = {10,1} and the equations are 2B = E + A, 2D = A + B, 2E = B + C, 2A = C + D, 2C = D + E. Their solutions mod 7 are a(1,1,1,1,1) + b(0,1,3,4,2): 49 colourings.

Certificates

For a returned word the program picks a regular projection, builds the c × c Fox matrix F mod 7, and finds by Gaussian elimination a matrix L with c − 1 rows such that row k of LF is ek − e0. It then checks LF ≡ E. Any colouring x has Fx ≡ 0, so (LF)x ≡ 0, so xk ≡ x0 for all k. The elimination is untrusted; only the multiplication check matters, and its soundness is the Lean theorem colorCheck_sound.

Seven colours

Draw the knot flat and cut it at each under-crossing into arcs. Paint every arc one of seven colours, numbered 0 to 6. The rule: at each crossing, twice the over-arc's number equals the sum of the two under-arcs' numbers, counting in sevens.

Painting everything one colour always works. For 5₂ there are other ways, 49 in all, and any drawing of 5₂ has them.

Click an arc to change its colour. A crossing turns solid when its rule holds.

Commentary

Fox 7-colourings correspond to homomorphisms from the knot group to the dihedral group D₇ sending meridians to reflections (Wirtinger presentation; rbrarb = r2b−a). So their number is a knot invariant, also for the mirror image. On the standard diagram the solutions are a(1,1,1,1,1) + b(0,1,3,4,2).

Every suspect has an alibi

This 24-step walk obeys all seven rules, so the search found it. It is a trefoil: with three colours it has nine colourings. With seven it has only the seven single-colour ones, so it cannot be 5₂.

The same holds for every walk the search found. Each comes with a short certificate, a small matrix whose product with the colouring rules forces all arcs to share one colour.

Commentary

For each word: a regular projection, the c × c Fox matrix F mod 7, and an untrusted elimination giving L with (LF) row k ≡ ek − e₀. Only the product LF is checked. Lean: colorCheck_sound.

What the computer checked

The whole argument is written in Lean, a language in which a proof is a program and a small trusted kernel checks it. The normal form, the completeness of the actual search program and the colouring certificates are all checked theorems.

What a reader still trusts: a few pages of definitions, two classical facts about knots, and that the compiled program evaluated 1358 true-or-false questions correctly.

Commentary

Making of. One early version of the search tested the corner rule one vertex too early. Its output matched the C++ program's, but its node counts did not, and the completeness proof could never have gone through.

The program is the proof object

Completeness is proved about the very program that was run. The search is written in a functional style the kernel can reason about, with walk data in immutable lists and explicit structural recursion on the remaining steps, while keeping a single mutable occupancy array that the compiler updates in place. It is a line-by-line equivalent of the independent C++ program and visits exactly the same number of nodes.

The moves

Flip a corner. Two perpendicular steps swap order and the corner swings across its unit square. Allowed when the fourth corner of the square is empty: click a ring. Cut a U-turn. Three sides of a square become the fourth: click a hatched square. Push out. The reverse of a cut, two steps longer; the tool for getting out of a corner.

Why the knot never changes

A unit lattice segment meets a closed unit lattice square in nothing, a corner or a whole side. So the square of a move meets the knot only in the edges being replaced, and each move is two Δ-moves across the square's triangles. The proof of the moves.

What the numbers mean

The shadow is a projection of the knot; its crossings change as the knot moves. Its Fox colourings do not: 9 three-colourings for the trefoil, 25 five-colourings for 4₁ and 5₁, 49 seven-colourings for 5₂. Why the count cannot change.

How the search grows

Every two steps of length multiply the search by about six. No polygon of length 22 or less satisfies C1–C7; the first words appear at length 24, and they are trefoils.

Where it ran

Lengths up to 32 ran partly in a 4-core container; length 34 (1024 parts) and half of length 32 run on GitHub Actions, which offers twenty free 4-core runners at a time to public repositories. At about 3 × 10⁵ nodes per second per core, a part of length 34 takes 40–60 minutes.

Provenance

A part is accepted only if its log records exit status 0, its output ends with the RESULT line for that part with the right number of words before it, the output's SHA-256 matches the logged one, and the program was built from sources identical to the current ones. Every part is deterministic, so anyone can re-run it and compare hashes.

Independent cross-checks

None is needed for the proof; each tests it. The C++ enumerator returns the same words at every length and, for unsplit runs, the same node counts. A Python count finds exactly 7 colourings for every word. The flip closure of the 50 trefoils at length 24 is the full census of 3328 shortest trefoils. An adversarial referee tested the colouring hypothesis on 12 750 diagrams of sixty 5₂ polygons.

How we got here

The first answer came from a fast search with extra lemmas, which suggested Len(5₂) = 36. Then the goal changed: a proof nobody could poke holes in.

That meant dropping every lemma we could not check, writing the search as a program Lean could reason about, letting an adversarial referee attack each draft, and rerunning everything under a provenance check.

Commentary

Two review rounds (round 1: a pipeline that could accept truncated output, unlisted glue code, citations of the hard direction of Reidemeister's theorem; round 2: local provenance, run traceability, the upper-bound trust list). All were fixed before the final runs.

What comes next

The faster, unverified version of the search, with the plane-crossing lemma for prime knots and a computer-checked arc lemma for composites, has been run to length 36, where it finds only the unknot, 3₁, 4₁, 5₁ and 5₂. This indicates that every other knot needs at least 38 steps; making it formal needs both lemmas in Lean. Reaching length 40, which would settle 6₁, 6₂, 6₃ and 3₁#3₁, all believed to need 40, needs roughly fifty times the present computation, or a smarter normal form.
