## Abstract
Recent attempts to train AI models to recognize knots have produced millions of "hard" unknot diagrams resistant to simplification by Reidemeister moves, pass moves, or random walks on the Reidemeister graph. Most are easy for the methods based on triangulations of the knot complement found in Regina and SnapPy, but more difficult for simplifiers based on diagrammatic moves. We present ReAPR (Re-embedding And Pass Rerouting), a semi-diagrammatic simplifier which alternates pass-move reduction on a knot diagram with a geometric re-embedding step. The re-embedding minimizes the total variation of a height function on the diagram subject to crossing constraints. We show that for an n-crossing diagram, the minimum total variation is 2(n − k), where k is the least number of crossings one must virtualize to make the diagram virtually alternating; this is a combinatorial invariant of the diagram. Reprojecting the resulting embedding from a new viewpoint reveals previously hidden simplifications. ReAPR successfully simplifies every published hard-unknot example we are aware of, as well as several new collections (≈ 2.6 million examples in total) in under 30 seconds of total CPU time, making it as effective as the best non-diagrammatic unknot recognition method (SnapPy) and about 60× faster. ReAPR is just as effective at simplifying diagrams of random knots, where it is regularly used to simplify diagrams with tens of millions of crossings.

## Notation and conventions
- (p. 1) "an n-crossing knot diagram D as a directed, 4-valent, embedded planar graph G(D) with over and under information at the crossings. The edges of this graph are arcs of the knot a_0, …, a_{2n−1}; we assume that they are numbered in the order in which we visit them." (Symbols a_i, G(D) inferred from context.)
- (p. 2) Maximal overpass = consecutive arcs a_1,…,a_m where the head of each a_i with i<m goes over at the corresponding crossing while the tail of a_1 and head of a_m go under; maximal underpass analogously. Pass move = rerouting an over/underpass; reducing pass move if the new path crosses ℓ < m−1 faces; pass graph = graph of diagrams connected by pass moves.
- (p. 5) Total variation TV(f) = Σ |f(a_{i+1}) − f(a_i)| around the closed diagram (Definition 1), f = height assigned to arcs; constraint f(over arc) − f(under arc) ≥ 1 at each crossing.
- (p. 6) Tension graph Γ(D) with 4n vertices (v_i, w_i) and 5n edges (Definition 2); matching edges / cycle edges; cost c(e) and lower bound ℓ(e) functions (2).
- (p. 9) Virtual crossing; virtualization; virtually alternating diagram; k = minimum number of crossings that must be virtualized to make D virtually alternating.
- (p. 10) "Lattice embedding": the diagram is embedded by an orthogonal planar grid layout (Tamassia–Tollis) plus a piecewise-constant height function (cubic lattice presentation); "cr." in Table 1 = average crossing number of the test-set diagrams.
- Effectiveness in Table 1 = fraction of unknots identified (1.0 = all). "darc" = directed arc; left(·), reverse(·) operators (p. 3).

**Lemma 3 (p. 6).** If f is an optimal solution to (3), then f(w_i) = max(f(v_i), f(v_{i+1})), and therefore the sum over e ∈ {v_i → w_i, v_{i+1} → w_i} of c(e)(f(head(e)) − f(tail(e))) is given by |f(v_i) − f(v_{i+1})|. **Lemma 4 (p. 6).** If f ∈ R^{4n} is an optimal solution of (3), then the cost of f is equal to TV(f). Further, f(head(e)) − f(tail(e)) ≥ ℓ(e) for all edges of Γ(D). Therefore, solving (3) is equivalent to solving (1). (Proved p. 7.)

**Proposition 6 (p. 7).** In an n-crossing diagram D, any height function f solving (1) has TV(f) ≤ 2n. Moreover, D is alternating if and only if TV(f) = 2n.
Proof idea: the height function (4) (0 for outgoing underarc, 1 for outgoing overarc) is feasible with TV = number of over/under switches ≤ 2n; for alternating D a dual-feasible flow of value 2n certifies optimality.

**Lemma 7 (p. 8).** There exists an optimal solution to (5) where each edge carries flow of 0 or 2. Proof idea: constraint matrix is a (totally unimodular) incidence matrix; halve supplies/demands to ±1 and apply Hoffman–Kruskal.

**Proposition 8 (p. 9) — proved here.** Suppose that D is an n-crossing diagram and k is the smallest number for which we can convert D into a virtually alternating diagram by virtualizing k crossings. Any optimal solution to (1) has TV(f) = 2(n − k). There is a corresponding height function with no more than n − k maxima and n − k minima.
Proof idea (as given): flow edges (matching edges carrying flow 2) must alternate between incoming and outgoing around the cycle (else a region would receive +4 but absorb only +2), hence correspond to a virtually alternating subset; maximum flow = 2(n − k) by duality; complementary slackness shows incoming/outgoing vertices are weak local maxima/minima of f.

## Key equations and quantitative claims outside numbered statements
- (p. 2) Minimising TV gives "vertically simplest" presentation; TV(f) = ∫ (number of times the curve crosses horizontal plane at height t) dt. The minimisation is a min-cost-flow problem solvable "in near-linear time in the number of crossings"; with integral bounded data, O(n^{1+o(1)}) by [26] (p. 5); in practice network simplex (MCFSimplex, [27]).
- (p. 4) Bidirectional BFS with smallest-frontier heuristic gave "roughly a 20× improvement over unidirectional BFS on large diagrams"; on a regular d-dim grid strict alternation visits a factor 2^{1−d} fewer vertices.
- (p. 4–5) Complexity discussion: reducing pass discovery in dual graph at worst O(n) per pass; O(n²) to rule out existence of any reducing pass; Reidemeister graph edges are found in O(n) but search radius may be large.
- (p. 10) Test sets (≈ 2.6 million unknot diagrams, 5 to 130,155 crossings; deposited on Dryad [21]): (A) 200 random lattice unknots filling spheres, created by BFACF [30–32]; (B) the 18 unknots among the "children's game" knots of Gukov et al. [3]; (C) 200 self-avoiding unknotted 10,000-gons in tight spherical confinement [33]; (D) 5 complicated "hard Goeritz" unknots from [15]; (E) the 21 "hard unknot" examples in [15]; (F) 27 rational-tangle unknots from Kauffman's challenge [34]; (G) 2,623,203 pass-reduced "hard unknots" found by Applebaum et al. [2]; (H) 600 unknotted random equilateral 3043-gons [35]; (I) 176 pass-reduced unknots by Tuzun–Sikora [17]; (J) 384 pass-reduced "very hard unknots" from [2]. Total 2,624,834 diagrams.
- (p. 11) Comparators: Regina Link.simplify() (random RIII + RI/RII); SnapPy Link.simplify('global') (pass moves + random RIII, default limit 100 random RIII moves; Link.deconnect_summand() used); non-diagrammatic: SnapPy fundamental group (random tetrahedralisations until < 10 tetrahedra then check relators, procedure suggested by N. Dunfield), Regina triangulation (isSolidTorus(); conclusive proof either way) and Regina fundamental group. Randomized, reseeded per diagram. Hardware: single core of a 2022 Mac Studio (Apple M1 Ultra). Time limits for Regina/SnapPy: 100 s per knot for set G, 1000 s for set A, 3000 s for other sets; 4 GB memory per knot (1 GB for SnapPy diagrammatic on sets A, B, D, H, I, J, never reached); no limits needed for ReAPR.
- (p. 11) "ReAPR and the SnapPy fundamental group method both achieved perfect effectiveness on every test set, and the Regina fundamental group method did so on every test set except A." Regina triangulation: 110 pass-reduced examples in G timed out at 100 s (≈ two thirds of its runtime on G). Regina's Link.simplify() "made almost no progress on test sets G and J"; SnapPy's diagrammatic method failed on larger pass-reduced examples in J.
- (p. 11) Test set A (200 lattice unknots, average ≈ 111K crossings): both Regina non-diagrammatic methods and SnapPy diagrammatic ran out of time on all; spot check: building the first triangulation took ≈ 30 hours for one knot (2 tetrahedra result); projected ≈ 0.7 years CPU for the whole set for each Regina method, ≈ 1 year for SnapPy diagrammatic. SnapPy fundamental group method processes set A in under 10 minutes (9.30m), ReAPR in under 10 seconds (8.65s). "in [38], we needed to simplify diagrams with up to 35 million crossings."
- (p. 12) Set F: Regina triangulation method could not recognize two of the 27 diagrams; fundamental group method took 30 min vs under a second for SnapPy's. For the four largest F diagrams (113 to 175 crossings), first triangulations have 100–200 tetrahedra; in 20 trials SnapPy's randomize() brought every triangulation to 2–7 tetrahedra within ≤ 73 calls, while 100 calls of Regina's simplify() barely reduced it (161–185 tetrahedra for the largest after 11 builds and 100 calls); isSolidTorus() exceeded 4 GB within seconds. Kauffman's Set F unknots can be simplified monotonically by pass moves, so easy for pass-move methods.
- (p. 13) Literature on energy-flow unknotting: Noma et al. [42] "requires several seconds to untangle the 32 crossing Freedman–He–Wang unknot"; such methods "are often defeated by test set F"; not tested.
- (p. 13) A 42-crossing hard unknot of Applebaum et al. [2] that "resisted 10^4 attempts to simplify it using SnapPy's 'global' heuristic" is simplified by ReAPR in 0.36 ms on a 2025 laptop.
- (p. 2) Known: any unknotting algorithm using Reidemeister moves must sometimes add crossings [15]; any using pass moves must sometimes use non-crossing-decreasing pass moves [16,17]; unknown whether a weakly decreasing sequence of pass moves always exists; for grid diagrams a weakly monotonic simplifying sequence exists [18] (Dynnikov).

Bidirectional search "Dijkstra with unit weights" growing the ball with the smaller frontier; rerouting reuses memory because ℓ < m−1.

## Methods / algorithms
ReAPR (Re-embedding And Pass Rerouting) (pp. 2–10; code Knoodle [20]).
1. (Sec. 2, pp. 2–5; App. A) Pass reduction: repeatedly find maximal over/underpasses; delete the pass from the diagram to get D̂ with two distinguished faces; find shortest path between them in the dual graph D̂* by bidirectional BFS (smallest-frontier heuristic) navigated implicitly via darc operators left(·) and reverse(·); if path length ℓ < m−1 reroute (reducing pass move); iterate greedily until no reducing pass move remains. No guarantee of optimality (greedy).
2. (Sec. 3.1, p. 5) Horizontal re-embedding: orthogonal layout of the planar 4-valent graph by Tamassia–Tollis [23] with turn-regularization / face saturation [24], minimum bends via network flow (network simplex MCFSimplex [27]).
3. (Sec. 3.2, pp. 5–10) Vertical re-embedding: choose piecewise-constant height function f minimising TV(f) subject to over-arc height ≥ under-arc height + 1 at each crossing; solved as minimum cost tension problem on the 4n-vertex, 5n-edge tension graph; solved via dual max cost flow (5); min TV = 2(n − k) (Prop. 8).
4. (p. 2, 10) Reproject the resulting lattice embedding from a new viewpoint to get a new diagram, often revealing new pass-move simplifications; alternate with pass reduction (finishes when a full round makes no change).
5. Evaluation (Sec. 4): see test sets and comparators above; metric: fraction of diagrams recognized as the unknot ("effectiveness") and total CPU time (single core). ReAPR is one-sided (can only certify unknot, via reduction to 0 crossings); verification is that the final diagram has 0 crossings.

## Stated limitations, open problems, future work
- "The most pressing mathematical question about ReAPR is the simplest: why does it work?" Mid-level pass moves (Petronio–Zanellati) accounted for "most, but not all" of ReAPR's simplifications; "there appear to be cases requiring coordinated rearrangement of larger portions of the diagram" (p. 13).
- "While we do not claim that ReAPR solves the unknot recognition problem … we would welcome examples on which it fails" (p. 3).
- Pass reduction is greedy; no guarantee the pass-reduced diagram is best (p. 2–3). Unknown whether a weakly decreasing sequence of pass moves always exists (p. 2).
- Suggestion: random crossing-number-preserving pass moves might improve simplifiers including ReAPR (p. 13).
- Energy-flow unknotting methods were not tested (p. 13); some other simplification strategies untested because implementations unmaintained (p. 12).
